### The Birds That Almost Never Land

`passage-swifts`

> Of all the birds that visit Britain each summer, the swift is the one most people have seen without ever knowing what they were looking at. It is a dark, scythe-shaped bird, screaming in parties around rooftops on warm evenings in June. It is often mistaken for a swallow or a house martin, and it is neither.
>
> What makes the swift remarkable is not its speed, though it is fast, but the sheer amount of its life spent in the air. A young swift leaving its nest in a British roof may not touch down again for two or even three years. In that time it will feed, drink, gather nesting material and sleep entirely on the wing, and it will fly to Africa and back several times before it ever perches on anything.
>
> Sleeping in flight sounds impossible, and for a long time nobody quite believed it. The evidence came from pilots, who reported seeing swifts at extraordinary heights at night, and later from tiny tracking devices light enough to be carried without harming the bird. Swifts climb to around two thousand metres at dusk, and are thought to rest one half of the brain at a time while continuing to fly.
>
> Their numbers in Britain have fallen sharply, by more than half since the middle of the 1990s. The reason is unusually simple for a conservation problem: swifts nest in small gaps under the eaves of older buildings, and modern roof repairs seal those gaps up. A bird that returns each May to the exact same crevice may find, one year, that there is no longer a crevice there.
>
> This also makes the problem unusually easy to help with. A swift box fixed high on a wall, or a special brick built into a new house, costs very little and lasts for decades. It is not often that saving a species is a matter of leaving a hole in a wall — but for the swift, very largely, it is.
>

### Why the Local Library Still Matters

`passage-library`

> Whenever a council announces that it is closing a library, somebody will say that libraries have had their day. Everything is online now, the argument runs; a building full of paper books is a pleasant memory rather than a public necessity. The argument sounds modern and sensible. It is also, on inspection, mostly wrong.
>
> Consider first who actually uses a library. It is not, in the main, people choosing between a borrowed novel and a bought one. It is a child doing homework in a warm room because the house is crowded; a jobseeker who needs a computer and an hour of quiet to fill in a form; a new parent at a rhyme-time session, talking to another adult for the first time that week. None of these needs is met by a website.
>
> Supporters of closure often point to the cost. Yet the sums involved are strikingly small — a typical library service costs a household a few pounds a year, less than a single paperback. Set against that is the cost of what replaces it, which is usually nothing, and which falls hardest on the households least able to make up the difference themselves.
>
> It is fair to say that libraries must change, and the best of them have. They now lend tools, run coding clubs, host warm spaces in winter and offer free access to newspapers and research databases that would cost an individual hundreds of pounds. A library that stood still would indeed deserve the accusation of being a museum. Very few have stood still.
>
> The real question is not whether we can afford libraries but what kind of place we want a town to be. A library is one of the last buildings a person can enter, stay in for several hours, and leave again without being expected to spend a penny. Once such a place is gone, it is almost never rebuilt.
>
